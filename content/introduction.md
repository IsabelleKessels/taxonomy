## Context

Philosophical theorising faces a fundamental obstacle: We rely on intuitions about thought experiments, but these intuitions frequently contradict our theories. In epistemology, complex thought experiments proliferate, and attempting to perfectly fit a theory to all our intuitions would bring theory-construction to a standstill. In the book _Operationalising Theory-Construction: Groundwork Towards a Taxonomy of Thought Experiments_, I offer a framework for assessing which intuitions hold evidential weight, and which may legitimately be set aside: Only sufficiently strong and stable intuitions, arising from relatively simple thought experiments, should inform theory-construction and -testing. To employ these criteria, however, thought experiments need to be taxonomised. Specifically, I suggest that they should be sorted into [case-types] and broader {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}.{{< fn 1 "To provide a visual marker, I use small caps for {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} and square brackets for [case types]." >}} A [case-type] groups together thought experiments with an analogous structure. If a [type] is adequately formulated, its tokens should evoke a uniform evaluation-behaviour,{{< fn 2 "I classify evaluation-behaviour as uniform if one of three patterns emerges: either (i.) all tokens evoke an inter- and intra-individually stable intuition of knowledge; or (ii.) all tokens evoke an inter- and intra-individually stable intuition of lack of knowledge; or (iii.) all tokens evoke an intuition that is subject to significant inter- and/or intra-individual instability, or an inter- and intra-individually stable intuition falling into the neutral area of neither knowledge, nor lack thereof. (See subchapter 4.2.3. of *Operationalising Theory-Construction* for more detail.)" >}} and data on intuitions about a number of its tokens can be used to make predictions about anticipated intuitions on further tokens. The {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} of a taxonomy identify common complicating factors within [types] of) thought experiments. They group together [case-types], and their respective case-tokens. The sorting into {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} allows us to distinguish levels of case-complexity:

- A single {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} labelled _‘{{< sc >}}Paradigmaticity{{< /sc >}}’_ subsumes all of the most basic [case-types] involving no complicating factor.
- Elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} subsume [case-types] and case-tokens employing exactly one complicating factor.
- Complex {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} subsume [case-types] and case-tokens combining two or more complicating factors represented in the elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}.

A {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}}’s _level of case-complexity_ is determined by the number of complicating factors combined in it. Thought experiments and [case types] subsumed by an _n-level {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}}_ thereby themselves have n level case-complexity. I argue that cases of high complexity are – on average – longer and more convoluted than those of low complexity, and we can reasonably expect that they are more likely to cause reasoning mistakes amongst those considering them. Therefore, case-complexity should be a criterion for assessing the evidential weight we grant to intuitions about a case, especially since – as is indicated by my study of thought experiments on _knowledge_ – there are relevant differences in case-complexity levels across thought experiments.

The present supplementary material serves as _proof of concept_ to the argument presented in the book: It demonstrates that building a taxonomy to the specifications I identify _is possible_. Moreover, it illustrates _significant differences in case-complexity levels_ discernible amongst the thought experiments on propositional knowledge advanced in epistemology.

## Structure of the material

The material is structured in four sections, reflecting the taxonomy’s different complexity-levels: Section A. presents the nought-level complexity {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} of {{< sc >}}Paradigmaticity{{< /sc >}} and the [case-types] falling under it. Section B. presents the taxonomy’s elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}; section C. identifies the resulting second-level complexity {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}. For each elementary {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} (B.1.-B.6.), I briefly characterise the complicating factor; for each second-level complexity {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} (C.1.-C.15.), I describe, where applicable, limitations to combining the two factors in question. Section D. gives some examples of thought experiments (and the [case-types] they fall under) which belong into second-level complexity {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}.

Within each section, the [case-types] subsumed by any {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} are presented by stating: (i.) their _[case-type]-formulation_, (ii.) one or more _token-cases_, and (iii.) a _commentary_. Where applicable, the commentary reports the author of a token-case’s stated intuition and/or x phi data on the token. In some cases, it gives more general information on the taxonomising process.

## Uses and limitations

The taxonomy has two limitations. Firstly, it aims at an adequate picture of philosophers’ opinions on cases in the relevant literature, incorporating existing data of x phi studies wherever available. It cannot, however, confirm its proposed [case-types] through x phi methods.{{< fn 3 "In subchapter 4.2.3. of *Operationalising Theory-Construction*, I argue for why this additional step should be taken." >}} Consequently, the [case type]-formulations below should be seen as _candidates_; further empirical testing on tokens is required to evidence uniform evaluation-behaviour. This is a function of x-phi’s relative youth, and a limitation I hope to be addressed as it matures. I see my contribution to this project as providing numerous indications of where further empirical investigation might be best placed.

Secondly, given the vast epistemological literature,{{< fn 4 "At the time of writing (November 2024), the topic ‘Epistemology’ subsumes over 57.000 entries at PhilPaper.org, of which slightly over 18.000 are grouped under the sub-topic ‘Knowledge’ (PhilPapers n.d.). Given that thought experiments relevant to knowledge are likely to occur across several sub-disciplines of ‘Epistemology’, it is not implausible that relevant cases are better counted in thousands than dozens." >}} it would be unrealistic to pretend I present every ([type] of) thought experiment. I, like everyone else, have a particular research-history and -interests. I am not so presumptuous as to claim either exhaust any field of study. A more comprehensive taxonomy than the one presented here requires not only empirical research, but a collective effort from the armchair; with other researchers adding case-tokens, and possibly [case-types], that I have missed. With this in mind, my preliminary taxonomy is licensed under Open Access [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) ![](/img/cc.svg)![](/img/by.svg) and the source material for this website can be accessed [here](https://github.com/IsabelleKessels/taxonomy "here"). This format allows others to create their own working branches of the taxonomy, and thereby adapt or expand my argument. I warmly invite you to join me in this exercise of public philosophy.

In the present, preliminary version, I am as thorough as possible in my treatment of {{< sc >}}Paradigmaticity{{< /sc >}} and elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}; identifying [case-types] and formulating my own token cases wherever I’m unaware of examples from armchair or empirical literature. For some second-level case-complexity {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}, there are only limited ways in which complicating factors can be combined; allowing for a full account of their subsumed [case-types]. For other second-level {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}, an exhaustive account of all conceivable [case-types] from the get-go would be too demanding. Here, I identify exemplary [case-types] and tokens based on thought experiments found in the literature, and explore the most obvious ways in which complicating factors might be combined. This explorative side of taxonomising identifies hitherto unformulated thought experiments of (at least) second-level case-complexity which might potentially decide conflicts between competing theorists.{{< fn 5 "See, e.g., C.7., [Counterfactually Not Gettiered] and [Actually and Counterfactually Gettiered]. I believe that investigating tokens of each potentially allows us to arbitrate between critics and proponents of modal (*safety* or *anti-luck*) theories of knowledge." >}} As with the progression of the x phi research project, this taxonomy can (and should) be amended as tokens of so-far unidentified [case-types] emerge in the epistemological literature. Again, I see the role of the taxonomy as spurring such enquiry, rather than passively awaiting it.

Although the inclusion of complicating factors is limited by operational concerns,{{< fn 6 "For example, I exclude thought experiments based on questions of personal identity – e.g., replica scenarios such as Swampman (Davidson 1987, 443f.). Epistemologists’ interpretations of such cases depend heavily on how they are evaluated from the metaphysical perspective of personal identity; and it is inadvisable to isolate any epistemological treatment from metaphysical commitments. The same applies for thought experiments addressing paradoxes of time travel. How epistemological questions raised in such cases should be handled depends greatly on premises outside of epistemology’s scope, rendering an independent account impossible." >}} the elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} proposed in the present version (see section B.) represent large swathes of the thought experiments that played an influential role in Post-Gettier theory-construction. The thought experiments, drawn from armchair philosophy papers, which I classify as having third-level case-complexity (see section D.) strikingly illustrate that combining complicating factors (characterising the elementary {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}) _produces relevant complex {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}}_ (i.e., {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} that aren’t empty). Taken together with examples provided in sections B. and C., this confirms that thought experiments on knowledge significantly differ in their case-complexity, and thereby strengthens my argument that case-complexity is a relevant factor to consider when assessing intuitions’ evidential weight.

## How to cite this taxonomy

If you’d like to reference the taxonomy or the website as a whole, please use the following format:

Keßels, I. (2026). *A Preliminary Taxonomy of Thought Experiments on Knowledge.* Knowledge Taxonomy. https://isabellekessels.github.io/taxonomy/


If you’d like to citing a {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} or [case-type], please use this format:

Keßels, I. (2026). *Category number. Name of {{< sc >}}Category{{< /sc >}} **or** name of [case-type].* Knowledge Taxonomy. URL of the Category
