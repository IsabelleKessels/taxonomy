title: Counterfactual-Interference
---

{{< sc >}}Counterfactual-Interference{{< /sc >}} is an interference with S’s evidence for p (and/or the matter of fact described by p) which would occur in modally close possible worlds, but not in the case’s @-world. There are two variants of this modal profile (see table 3). Cases with Modal Profile A are often labelled epistemic Frankfurt cases’ – indicating that agents _cannot but believe_ that p, no matter p’s truth value.{{< fn 1 "The label alludes to Frankfurt’s (1969) famous counterexample to the claim that the possibility that one *could have acted differently* is necessary for moral responsibility. Agents in the epistemic ‘equivalents’ might be able to *do nothing* in the modally close possible worlds, and form *no belief* pertaining to whether p. However, it’s typically presupposed that they aim to find out whether or not p, and that nothing makes them withhold judgement. Philosophers who use this label for cases with this modal profile include Zagzebski (2001) and Kelp (2009; 2016; 2017). Keßels (2022) argues that the label is misleading, indicating significant disanalogies between the structure of these cases and that of the original Frankfurt case. Pritchard (2015) offers the label of ‘modal environmental epistemic luck’ (contrasted to ‘environmental epistemic luck’, see the introduction to B.3., fn. 1, and ‘intervening epistemic luck’ see the introduction to B2, fn. 2) for the type of luck in place in such cases. He claims that “cases of modal environmental epistemic luck are effectively *epistemic Frankfurt-style cases*.” (106), but I’d argue that Pritchard’s label of ‘modal environmental luck’ fits both cases of Modal Profile A and B. Pritchard himself doesn’t spell out what exact modal profile he uses as the basis for his generalisation." >}} The analogy to ‘Frankfurt cases’ breaks down for cases with Modal Profile B. What unites cases of both modal profiles is that, in their @-worlds, S believes that p based on _prima facie_ suitable evidence with no misleading evidence present; but in modally close possible worlds S would form a false belief based on misleading evidence. Tokens of [types] of both profiles will differ in:

- the modal closeness to the @-world of the possible worlds in which the interference would occur,
- the rate of interference to non-interference worlds, and
- the nature of the counterfactual-interference, i.e., a supernatural event, or an unlikely (albeit nomologically possible) event.{{< fn 2 "Consider, e.g., the two tokens for [Instrument Counterfactual-Interference in Modally Close non-p-Worlds] for an example of how this may influence evaluation behaviour: Kelp’s (2009) Demon Clock 1 involves an evil demon, whereas Bogardus’s (2014) Atomic Clock token features a somewhat more realistic counterfactual-interference. Participants might plausibly perceive possible worlds featuring a demon’s interference to be relatively remote from the @ world, even if the author of the case stipulates that *the demon is present*, albeit remaining inactive, in the @-world serving as our reference point." >}}

Since the first two factors significantly influence the epistemic risk of gaining a false belief, and the third factor may have significant influence on a participant’s perspective on the case-token, formulating [sub-types] may be required to achieve uniform evaluation behaviour.

For both modal profiles, the basis of belief in the @-world is an instance of [Clear Perception] and/or [Correct Reasoning From True Premises] relating to what S is perceiving in the moment, i.e., where the evidence functions as a true premises of S’s reasoning process. I exclude [Accurate Memory], because the belief must be formed (at least partially) on evidence gained in the present moment of the @-world (see the comment to this [case-type] in A.). Similarly, instances of [Correct Reasoning From True Premises] which are not based on experience at all are excluded. The same applies in complex {{< sc >}}Categories{{< /sc >}} involving this complicating factor. In the counterfactual-scenarios it would be instances of [Misperception] and/or [False Reasoning], where misleading evidence functions as a false premise of S’s reasoning process.
